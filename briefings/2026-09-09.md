The controversy surfaces a more immediate design concern: when AI agents operate against knowledge that may overlap with third-party work conducted via the same provider's API, attribution and information-boundary questions become live risks. Any agentic system that ingests user-generated knowledge (support tickets, research notes, internal documents) needs a clear provenance model before it acts on that information externally.

### 2. Terence Tao: AI is mining open research problems non-renewably

**Source:** Simon Willison's Weblog (quoting Tao's ICM 2026 lecture) · **Published:** 2026-09-09 · **Read time:** ~3 min

**Elevator pitch:** Fields Medalist Terence Tao argues that AI is consuming the finite stock of good open mathematical problems faster than the community can replenish them, that even rumors of progress now trigger AI-powered races to close problems out, and that the incentive structure now points toward secrecy — reversing centuries of open science.

**Opinion vs. fact**

- [FACT] Tao delivered this argument as part of his ICM 2026 lecture; it is available as arXiv:2608.16753.
- [FACT] Tao states that "even the rumor of someone working on a problem can trigger a massive amount of AI-powered effort to flatten it before the original research project has time to reach its full potential."
- [OPINION] Tao argues good open problems are being "mined in a non-renewable fashion."
- [OPINION] Tao warns the incentive structure now favors not sharing promising research directions, which would "do serious long-term damage to the future of the field."
- [OPINION] Tao frames the future scarcity shift: proofs move from scarcity to abundance; validation, explanation, understanding, and community recognition become the new scarce resources.

**References:** Terence Tao (Fields Medalist, UCLA), ICM 2026 lecture, arXiv:2608.16753, Simon Willison.

**Justifications:** The argument that open problems are slow to replenish is well-grounded in how mathematics actually works — problems accumulate over decades and can't be manufactured. The Navier-Stokes announcement the same day provides an almost too-convenient example. The weakest point is the prediction that open science norms will collapse; historical counter-pressures (funding mandates, journal policies, community norms) are not addressed, and researchers have adapted to competitive pressures before.

**Core points**

- AI can now close out open problems at a speed that leaves human researchers unable to complete their own projects on related questions.
- The first-mover dynamic has changed: knowing someone is working on a problem is now sufficient reason to race AI agents at it.
- This creates perverse incentives: researchers working on promising directions face a choice between sharing early (risking pre-emption) or keeping work secret (reversing open science).
- Tao's framing for what becomes scarce in an answer-abundant world: validation, explanation, understanding, responsibility, and the ability to consolidate scattered results into stable theories.
- Published at ICM 2026; confirmed the same day by the Navier-Stokes announcement and Buckmaster's dispute with OpenAI.

**Impact:** For solutions architects designing AI systems in knowledge-intensive professional domains (legal research, scientific R&D, technical documentation), this is a signal about how domain experts will behave differently. Experts in fields with scarce, valuable open problems will become more protective of their working hypotheses. Systems that aggregate or act on domain expert knowledge need to anticipate that the supply of openly shared expert knowledge may shrink — both as a design constraint and as an IP risk management consideration.

**Elevator pitch:** AI coding tools have outpaced the engineering teams reviewing their output: review time has increased 440% at some firms, unreviewed code is merging by default, and the skill that matters most is now "knowing when not to trust AI-generated code" rather than writing it.

**Opinion vs. fact**

- [FACT] Faros AI 2026 study: median code review duration increased more than 440% as teams adopted AI coding tools.
- [FACT] Sonar study: 38% of developers say reviewing AI-generated code requires more effort than reviewing colleague-written code.
- [FACT] Industry data: unmanaged AI-generated code can drive maintenance costs to roughly 4× traditional levels within two years.
- [FACT] 81% of executives report technical debt is already constraining their AI initiatives.
- [OPINION] "The defining technical risk of the current moment is more code entering production with the same human review capacity" — framing from multiple industry analysts.
- [OPINION] Anders Hejlsberg (creator of C# and TypeScript): "AI is shifting the craft from writing code, to reviewing code."
- [OPINION] Teams adopting a "plan-and-review" model — architect, specify, AI generates, humans rigorously review — will get more durable value than teams who simply add AI to existing workflows.

**References:** Faros AI (2026 study), Sonar (developer survey), Anders Hejlsberg, academic paper "An Endless Stream of AI Slop" (arXiv:2603.27249).

**Justifications:** The 440% review-time increase is from an actual 2026 study with observable data, not anecdote. The "unreviewed code merges by default" claim aligns with the mechanics: if generation accelerates and review capacity is fixed, a backlog forms and organizations rationalize merging. The weakest point is that "plan-and-review" is prescribed without controlled comparison; we don't have data that this model recovers the productivity gains while maintaining quality.

**Core points**

- At companies with significant AI coding tool adoption, more code is entering review than teams can process — review has become the bottleneck, not generation.
- AI-generated code passes surface inspection but conceals subtle bugs, security vulnerabilities, and faulty assumptions that emerge after deployment.
- The industry is converging on a new workflow: engineers design architecture and specifications, AI generates code, engineers rigorously review — with the scarce skill being specification quality and review judgment, not typing speed.
- AI code review tools (PR integrations that auto-comment) are a partial answer; they need full-codebase context, low false-positive tuning, and outcome measurement rather than comment volume to add value.
- The 81% executive-reported technical debt constraint is the lagging indicator; the leading indicator is review queue depth growing faster than team capacity.

**Impact:** Direct and actionable for solutions architects managing engineering teams or designing AI-integrated development pipelines. The 440% review-time figure is not a temporary adjustment — it reflects the measurability gap described in item 4 below. Architecting for AI-heavy development means explicitly planning review capacity (tooling investment, process redesign, hiring for review skill) in proportion to generation capability, not as an afterthought. For telecom OSS teams where code correctness is critical, underweighting review capacity in AI adoption is a fast path to technical debt that compounds against reliability requirements.

### 4. Verification cost, not task complexity, is the new automation boundary

**Source:** Martin Fowler (Fragments) · **Published:** 2026-09-08 · **Read time:** ~3 min

**Elevator pitch:** Christian Catalini's economics of AGI frame a clean architectural insight: AI reduces generation cost exponentially while verification cost stays human-bottlenecked, so the real automation frontier is defined not by task difficulty but by measurability.

**Opinion vs. fact**

- [FACT] Catalini published this argument as "Some Simple Economics of AGI" (arXiv:2602.20946) with Xiang Hui (WashU).
- [FACT] The paper models two racing cost curves: exponentially decaying Cost to Automate vs. biologically bottlenecked Cost to Verify.
- [OPINION] Catalini argues the old automation boundary (routine vs. non-routine) has been replaced by a new one: measurable vs. non-measurable.
- [OPINION] This explains why AI first succeeded in chat, code, and image generation — outputs are easy to inspect, not because these were the hardest problems.
- [OPINION] As execution becomes a commodity, economic rents migrate to verification and accountability.

**References:** Christian Catalini (MIT), Xiang Hui (Washington University St. Louis), arXiv:2602.20946, Martin Fowler.

**Justifications:** The measurability framework is elegantly predictive — it accounts for the existing AI product landscape and gives a clean criterion for evaluating where agentic deployment is defensible. The strongest point is its explanatory power: "measurable" precisely characterizes why chat/code/images succeeded before, say, legal research or scientific hypothesis generation. The weakest point is treating verification cost as fixed — verification itself is increasingly automated (formal proofs, test suites, evals), which means the "biological bottleneck" claim may soften faster than the model assumes.

**Core points**

- Generation cost is dropping exponentially; verification cost is not. The gap between them is widening.
- Old automation boundary: routine vs. non-routine tasks. New boundary: measurable vs. non-measurable outputs — "can you tell if this is right?"
- First AI products succeeded where output inspection was cheap (read a sentence, look at an image, run a test), not where problems were hardest.
- As agents handle more execution, value concentrates in verification — the humans (and systems) that judge whether output is correct become the scarce resource.
- This framework connects directly to the code review crisis (#3 above): the 440% review-time increase is a measurability gap manifesting in production.

**Impact:** High architectural relevance. The measurability criterion gives a clean decision rule for agentic system design: automate confidently where output is measurable; if not, build measurability into the system (formal verification, structured outputs, test harnesses, evals) or keep humans in the loop. It also predicts where the next wave of AI tooling will concentrate: on the verification side of the gap — better evals, output validators, formal verification pipelines. For a solutions architect evaluating where to deploy AI agents in OSS or BSS workflows, "is the output measurable?" is a more reliable question than "is the task routine?"

- GPT-5.6 Sol is the model behind this; the Codex harness handled the lab hardware interface.

**Impact:** The operational pattern — agentic loop for well-defined measurement tasks, human escalation for ambiguous results — is directly transferable to other technical domains. In telecom OSS/BSS: AI agents could autonomously execute network health measurements, alarm correlation, routine fault isolation, and threshold calibration, with human escalation for novel or ambiguous patterns. The 90% autonomy rate in a highly technical domain with real hardware suggests this pattern is mature enough to architect against now, not in 2–3 years. The key design question is the same as Catalini's: can you measure whether the agent's output is correct cheaply enough to trust it without human review on every call?
